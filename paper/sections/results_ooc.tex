\subsection{Out of core: the leaf follows the device, and nesting pays only with the hierarchy paged}\label{sec:ooc}

The nested structures of Ogayar-Anguita et al.~\cite{ogayar2023nestedlidar} were measured in an out-of-core store where every node was a file, and the gains they reported were gains in node loads. Section~\ref{sec:composition} emulated a cost per node and found none; this section puts the leaves, and then the hierarchy, on a storage device. The engine's leaf store writes the reordered point arrays of every leaf to a file and reads them back on demand with direct I/O; the hierarchy is either kept in memory or read page by page, a page read for every sibling group a query tests; and an optional least-recently-used cache holds one percent of the store. Five regimes result: in memory (the control), leaves on the device with the hierarchy in memory, both paged, and the last two with the cache. Five 100M clouds, their pipeline traces and a workload of 30 large boxes per cloud were run through every regime on a panel of singles and nested schemas with leaves from 32 to 32\,768 points.

The device at hand, a consumer QLC solid-state drive that was almost full, did not time steadily: the same tree with the same reads took up to 3.5 times longer in one process than in another. Reads and bytes per query are exact, and the CPU part of a latency (the measured time minus the I/O time) is steady, so the I/O is modelled: each read costs a fixed latency plus its bytes at a bandwidth, for four device classes (an NVMe drive at 60\us{} and 3\,GB/s, a SATA drive at 120\us{} and 500\,MB/s, a hard disk at 8\,ms and 150\,MB/s, and the fit of the QLC drive at rest, 107\us{} and 47\,MB/s), and both workloads are reported on the modelled latency of their whole query mix. Stage A times the panel; stage B then tries nested prefixes of two to eight quadtree, octree or grid levels over each regime's best single, with the same adoption rule as the search: a prefix counts only if it is at least 3\% faster in every process.

\paragraph{With the hierarchy in RAM, nothing changes.} Table~\ref{tab:ooc} shows stage B adopting a nested prefix in \oocInRamAdopted{} of \oocInRamCells{} cells with the leaves on the device and the hierarchy in memory, on every device class, on the trace and on the boxes; the best prefix is within one to three percent of the single. What does change is the leaf. In memory the best singles have leaves of 32 to 128 points; with the leaves on an SSD they have 512 to 2\,048, on a hard disk 8\,192, and for the large boxes 32\,768. The leaf is the lever, and it follows the latency--bandwidth product of the device: a leaf should cost about as much to scan as a read costs to issue. The primitive stays: in every cloud the best single out of core has the primitive of the in-memory pick, the kd-tree on the ground and forest scans and the quadtree on the aerial ones, and at the device's leaf the rule's primitive is within a few percent of it.

\paragraph{With the hierarchy paged, a wide top pays in four cells.} When the hierarchy itself is read from the device, a nested prefix qualifies in \oocPagedAdopted{} of \oocPagedCells{} cells: on the SATA model with the cache, a quadtree prefix over the kd-tree on Lille and DomFountain, and on the QLC drive at rest without a cache, a two- or four-level quadtree over a hierarchical grid on Lille and Waikato. In each case the prefix spares hierarchy page reads of a binary tree: the gain is all in pages, not in work. On the NVMe and hard-disk models no prefix qualifies, on the first because reads are cheap and on the second because the leaf has grown until the hierarchy hardly matters. This is the regime of the 2023 study, and it is where its result reproduces: its nested schemas run in \isprsOverLeafMatchedMin{} to \isprsOverLeafMatchedMax{} of the time of their leaf-matched, depth-capped singles, while a single tuned for the device is \isprsOverBestSingleMin{} to \isprsOverBestSingleMax$\times$ faster than they are.

\paragraph{The price of paging the hierarchy.} A 100M-point hierarchy at the device's leaf is \hierarchyMbMin{} to \hierarchyMbMax\,MB. Keeping it in memory makes the best schema, nested or single, \pagedOverPinnedMinNoCache{} to \pagedOverPinnedMaxNoCache$\times$ faster than the best schema with the hierarchy paged and no cache, and \pagedOverPinnedMinCache{} to \pagedOverPinnedMaxCache$\times$ faster than with the one-percent cache. The one regime in which composition pays is therefore one that costs several times more than the alternative of pinning a few tens of megabytes, and the SATA case would also yield to a page-blocked layout of the pool, which is a layout change and not a second primitive. For comparison, reading the same boxes through a cloud-optimised point cloud reader (COPC) was about 26 times slower than the leaf store's best schema on the same drive, so the store is not a weak baseline for the regime.

\paragraph{Caveats.} The devices are modelled, not measured, and the drive at hand is steady only at rest; the cache is one policy at one size; the pool is laid out depth first, not in device pages; reads are synchronous at queue depth one, where a real store would prefetch or batch; and the CPU part was timed interleaved, which Section~\ref{sec:protocol} found up to a fifth slow, at most two percent of a modelled latency.
